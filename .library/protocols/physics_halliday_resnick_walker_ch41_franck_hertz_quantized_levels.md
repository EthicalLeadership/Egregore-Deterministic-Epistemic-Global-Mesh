# Protocol: James Franck & Gustav Hertz – *On Collisions between Electrons and Molecules of Mercury Vapor and the Ionization Potential of the Same* (1914)

**Chapter:** 41 – All About Atoms  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Recommended  
**Prerequisites:** Bohr model of the atom, discrete energy levels, inelastic collisions, operation of a vacuum triode, elementary gas discharge.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Franck and Hertz’s 1914 paper, “Über Zusammenstöße zwischen Elektronen und den Molekülen des Quecksilberdampfes und die Ionisierungsspannung desselben,” *Verhandlungen der Deutschen Physikalischen Gesellschaft* 16, 457–467.
- **Free access:** [IDC-online – English translation excerpt](https://www.idc-online.com/technical_references/pdfs/civil_engineering/The_Franck_Hertz_experiment_supports_Bohrs_model.pdf)
- **Translation notes:** The authors use “Ionisierungsspannung” (ionization potential) for what is actually the first excitation potential; “Molekülen” reflects contemporary usage for atoms/molecules of the vapor. Read with care: their interpretation differs from the modern one.

## 2. What the Authors Did NOT Have

| Authors Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Full Bohr–Sommerfeld quantum theory | Bohr’s 1913 model was new and not yet widely accepted; Franck and Hertz did not set out to test it. |
| Quantum mechanics / wavefunctions | Energy levels are inferred from collision thresholds, not from solving a wave equation. |
| Distinction between excitation and ionization | They interpreted the 4.9 V threshold as the ionization potential of mercury; it is actually the first excitation energy. |
| Electron spin and selection rules | Spin was unknown in 1914; metastable states and transition probabilities are absent from their analysis. |
| Modern vacuum-tube technology | The triode used a heated platinum filament, wire-mesh grid, and cylindrical anode; electron energies were controlled by a variable grid potential. |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Sketch the energy-level diagram of a mercury atom and calculate the photon wavelength corresponding to a 4.9 eV transition (answer: ≈ 253 nm, in the ultraviolet).
- [ ] **Primary source:** Read the experimental description: the apparatus, the heating of mercury vapor, the accelerating voltage between cathode and grid, and the small retarding potential between grid and anode.
- [ ] **Data:** Examine the plot of anode current vs. accelerating voltage. Identify the peaks and dips at roughly 4.9 V, 9.8 V, 14.7 V, etc.
- [ ] **Close reading:** Note that Franck and Hertz interpret the first drop as the onset of ionization. Contrast this with the modern interpretation: the electrons lose energy by inelastically exciting mercury atoms to the first excited state.
- [ ] **Derive:** Show that the spacing between current minima equals the first excitation energy and that the emitted UV line (253.7 nm) matches this energy.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Understand that Franck and Hertz were investigating gas-discharge phenomena and electron collisions, not explicitly testing Bohr’s model. Bohr himself pointed out in 1915 that their 4.9 V feature was excitation, not ionization.
- [ ] **Reception task:** The experiment became one of the most direct confirmations of discrete atomic energy levels and contributed to Franck and Hertz’s 1925 Nobel Prize “for their discovery of the laws governing the impact of an electron upon an atom.”

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

The Franck–Hertz experiment is now a standard undergraduate demonstration of quantised atomic excitation; the current minima occur when electrons have just enough kinetic energy to excite the first atomic level.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern experiment is designed to test Bohr’s theory and uses neon or mercury with a clear energy-level picture. Franck and Hertz were measuring what they thought was an ionisation potential; the interpretive framework had to be corrected by Bohr and others.

## 5. Secondary Sources

- A. P. French & E. F. Taylor, *An Introduction to Quantum Physics* – clear modern exposition.
- C. Gearhart, “James Franck and the Experimental Discovery of Metastable States” (*Physics in Perspective*, 2016) – nuance on the 1914 interpretation.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Saying Franck and Hertz “proved Bohr’s model” in 1914; they did not initially interpret their data that way.
- Confusing the 4.9 V excitation potential with the true ionisation potential of mercury (10.4 V).
- Assuming the experiment directly showed electron energy levels as we draw them today; it showed thresholds for inelastic energy loss, which were later mapped onto Bohr levels.

## 7. Follow-up / Cross-references

- Schrödinger 1926 wave mechanics protocol (the eventual theoretical replacement for Bohr levels).

- [ ] I can explain the difference between their 1914 interpretation (ionisation) and the modern interpretation (excitation).
- [ ] I can name what the modern energy-level diagram obscures about the original experiment’s interpretive uncertainty.
