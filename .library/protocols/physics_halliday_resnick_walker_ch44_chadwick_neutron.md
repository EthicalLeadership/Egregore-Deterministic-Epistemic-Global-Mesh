# Protocol: James Chadwick – *Possible Existence of a Neutron* (1932) and *The Existence of a Neutron* (1932)

**Chapter:** 44 – Nuclear Physics  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Recommended  
**Prerequisites:** Rutherford scattering, nuclear structure, conservation of energy and momentum in nuclear reactions, radioactivity (alpha, beta, gamma), mass-energy relation.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Read the two papers together. The *Nature* letter (27 February 1932) is the announcement; the *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* paper (1 June 1932) gives the full experimental argument and mass estimate.
- **Free access:**
  - *Nature* 129, 312 (1932) – [Nature PDF](https://www.nature.com/nature/journal/v129/n3252/pdf/129312a0.pdf)
  - *Proc. R. Soc. A* 136, 692–708 (1932) – [Cloudylabs mirror PDF](http://www.cloudylabs.fr/wp/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/The-existence-of-a-neutron-1932-Chadwick.pdf)
- **Translation notes:** Both papers are in English. Chadwick uses “neutron” for the new neutral particle; “beryllium radiation” refers to the penetrating radiation produced by α-particle bombardment of beryllium.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Quark model | The neutron is treated as an elementary particle; its internal structure (udd quarks) was unknown. |
| Weak interaction theory | Chadwick could not explain how a free neutron decays into a proton, electron, and antineutrino; beta decay remained puzzling. |
| Nuclear shell model | The neutron is invoked to solve the gross mass–charge problem of nuclei; detailed nuclear structure comes later. |
| Particle detectors beyond ionisation chambers and cloud chambers | Neutrons are detected indirectly through recoil protons and nuclear disintegrations; modern neutron detectors did not exist. |
| Pauli’s neutrino hypothesis (widely accepted) | The continuous beta spectrum is mentioned as a related puzzle, but the neutrino is not part of Chadwick’s argument. |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Write down the nuclear reaction for α particles (⁴He) striking beryllium-9 and predict the products using modern conservation laws. What neutral particle with mass ≈ 1 u would balance the equation?
- [ ] **Primary source (Nature letter):** Read the one-page letter. Identify the two key observations: (i) the beryllium radiation ejects protons from paraffin with unexpectedly high energy, and (ii) the radiation produces recoil nuclei in light elements.
- [ ] **Primary source (Proc. R. Soc.):** Read the sections on experimental arrangement and measurements. Note the use of a polonium α source, beryllium target, paraffin wax, and ionisation chamber.
- [ ] **Close reading:** Follow Chadwick’s argument that the observed proton energies are incompatible with a γ-ray hypothesis (conservation of energy and momentum would require an absurdly energetic photon).
- [ ] **Mass estimate:** Reproduce Chadwick’s calculation of the neutron mass from the recoil energies of protons and nitrogen nuclei. Note the assumption that the neutron mass is close to the proton mass.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Place Chadwick’s work in context: Bothe and Becker (1930) observed the penetrating radiation; the Joliot-Curies (1932) interpreted it as high-energy γ rays and missed the neutron; Chadwick, building on Rutherford’s earlier speculation, recognised the neutral-particle interpretation.
- [ ] **Reception task:** The neutron was rapidly accepted because it resolved the nucleus mass–charge discrepancy and explained the Joliot-Curie observations. It opened the door to Fermi’s neutron-induced radioactivity and, ultimately, nuclear fission.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

The modern picture is a neutron of mass 1.008665 u, spin ½, composed of one up and two down quarks, bound in the nucleus by the strong force and subject to weak beta decay (lifetime ≈ 880 s).

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern neutron is a composite particle undergoing weak decay. Chadwick’s neutron is an elementary, stable (for his purposes) neutral constituent of the nucleus; its decay, quark structure, and role in the weak interaction are entirely absent from his argument.

## 5. Secondary Sources

- A. Brown, *The Neutron and the Bomb: A Biography of Sir James Chadwick* – scientific and wartime context.
- F. Close, *Neutrino* – connects the neutron discovery to the larger story of weak interactions.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Saying Chadwick “discovered” the neutron in isolation; he reinterpreted observations by Bothe, Becker, and the Joliot-Curies, following Rutherford’s 1920 suggestion.
- Assuming Chadwick knew the neutron decays; free-neutron decay was established only later.
- Treating the neutron as a proton–electron composite in the modern sense; Chadwick considered this possibility, but it was superseded by the quark model and weak interaction theory.

## 8. Epistemic Safety Checklist (Post-Reading)

- [ ] I can state, in Chadwick’s own terms, why the beryllium radiation cannot be a γ ray.
- [ ] I can identify at least one tool or concept Chadwick lacked that I used to understand the neutron.
- [ ] I can describe the prior observations by Bothe, Becker, and the Joliot-Curies, and how Chadwick’s interpretation differed.
- [ ] I can name what the modern quark-and-weak-interaction picture obscures about Chadwick’s original neutral-particle argument.
- [ ] I have not used the phrase “basically the same as” or “just a primitive version of” in my notes.
