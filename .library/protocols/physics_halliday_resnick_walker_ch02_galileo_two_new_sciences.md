# Protocol: Galileo Galilei – *Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences* (1638)

**Chapter:** 2 – Motion Along a Straight Line  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Basic algebra; understanding of distance, time, velocity, and acceleration.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Crew & de Salvio translation (1914), public domain. Readable and faithful.
- **Free access:** [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/dialoguesconcerningtwonewsciences)
- **Alternate:** Stillman Drake translation (modern prose, not free).

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Algebraic notation | Geometry is his calculus; proportions replace equations |
| Precise clock | Time is measured by water flow, pendulum, or musical rhythm |
| Differential calculus | His *gradus velocitatis* (degree of velocity) is an operational, geometric concept of instantaneous speed — not a derivative, but not nonexistent |
| Concept of mass as inertial property | He reasons about speed and distance, not force/mass ratios |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Derive the odd-number rule (1, 3, 5, 7…) from \(s = \frac{1}{2}at^2\) for successive unit time intervals. Write it out step by step.
- [ ] **Primary source:** Open the *Dialogues*, Third Day, "On Naturally Accelerated Motion." Read from the beginning of the Third Day up to the proof of Theorem I (the mean speed theorem: a body in uniform acceleration covers the same distance as one moving at the mean velocity).
- [ ] **Close reading:** Continue to Theorem II (the odd-number law: distances fallen in successive equal time intervals are in the ratio of odd numbers). Focus on Salviati’s geometric proof. Do not skip the diagrams — they are the argument.
- [ ] **Re-derive:** Re-derive Theorem II algebraically and compare: what does the geometric proof make visible that the algebraic proof hides?
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Identify what Galileo is arguing against — Aristotelian proportionalities and the medieval mean speed theorem tradition.
- [ ] **Reception task:** Note that the *Two New Sciences* was published under house arrest; it circulated in manuscript before print. Its kinematics were absorbed gradually, not immediately.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

Galileo’s core result: starting from rest with uniform acceleration, distances in successive equal time intervals are proportional to 1, 3, 5, 7… This maps to the modern equation \(\Delta x = \frac{1}{2}a(2n-1)\) when \(t = 1\) unit.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The algebraic form treats time as a continuous variable and distance as a function; Galileo’s geometric proof treats time as a sequence of discrete intervals and distance as an accumulation of proportional areas. The modern form obscures the accumulative logic of his reasoning.

## 5. Secondary Sources

- Ernst Mach, *The Science of Mechanics* – historical analysis of Galileo’s contributions.
- Stillman Drake, *Galileo at Work* – deeper context on his experimental and geometric methods.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Reversing Theorem I and Theorem II; Theorem I is the mean speed theorem, Theorem II is the odd-number law.
- Treating geometric diagrams as deficient algebraic formulas; the diagrams are the proof.
- Claiming Galileo had “no concept of instantaneous velocity”; he had *gradus velocitatis*, a geometric, non-differential concept.
- Misinterpreting “accelerated motion” as including non-uniform acceleration; Galileo strictly defines it as equal increments of speed in equal times.

## 7. Follow-up / Cross-references

- Newton’s *Principia*, Axioms – see how Newton built on these kinematic foundations.
- Halliday’s derivation of the kinematic equations.

## 8. Epistemic Safety Checklist (Post-Reading)

- [ ] I can state Galileo’s theorems in his own geometric vocabulary, without using \(s = \frac{1}{2}at^2\).
- [ ] I can identify at least one tool Galileo lacked that I used to understand his result.
- [ ] I can describe how the *Two New Sciences* was received — not just its later canonization.
- [ ] I can name what the modern kinematic equations obscure about Galileo’s argument.
- [ ] I have not used the phrase “basically the same as” or “just a primitive version of” in my notes.
