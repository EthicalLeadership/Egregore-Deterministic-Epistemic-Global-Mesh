# Protocol: Sadi Carnot – *Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire* (1824)

**Chapter:** 20 – Entropy and the Second Law of Thermodynamics (Carnot part)  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Understanding of heat engines, the concept of reversibility, and the Kelvin-Planck statement of the Second Law.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Thurston translation (1890), public domain.
- **Free access:** [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/reflectionsonmot00carn)
- **Translation notes:** Carnot writes under the caloric theory; “chute de calorique” (fall of caloric) is analogous to a temperature difference driving work.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| First Law of Thermodynamics | Carnot assumes caloric is conserved in a cycle; he does not know heat and work are interconvertible |
| Concept of entropy | Entropy is Clausius’s 1865 invention; Carnot has no state function for irreversibility |
| Absolute temperature scale | He uses empirical temperatures (steam point, etc.); the functional form of efficiency is correct, but the temperature scale is not absolute |
| Concept of energy | “Motive power” is work output; “caloric” is a conserved heat fluid |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Sketch a heat engine operating between two reservoirs. Write down the Carnot efficiency formula from memory. Then set it aside — Carnot never wrote this formula with absolute temperature.
- [ ] **Primary source:** Read Carnot’s introduction and the description of the ideal heat engine (the Carnot cycle).
- [ ] **Cycle:** Identify the four steps of the cycle in his description (he does not label them isothermal/adiabatic in the modern sense).
- [ ] **Caloric reasoning:** Notice that Carnot assumes caloric is conserved in a cycle, yet he still correctly argues that maximum efficiency depends only on temperature. How? He uses a *reductio ad absurdum* based on the impossibility of perpetual motion.
- [ ] **Reversibility:** Work through his reasoning that reversibility guarantees maximum efficiency.
- [ ] **Critical distinction:** Caloric is the conserved heat fluid that passes through the engine, doing work as it “falls” from hot to cold. It is not a precursor to entropy. The modern analog of caloric in his reasoning is the quantity of heat (energy) that passes through the engine.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Identify what Carnot is arguing against — the inefficiency of contemporary steam engines and the lack of a theoretical limit on their performance.
- [ ] **Reception task:** Carnot’s work was largely ignored until Clapeyron (1834) and Clausius (1850) revived it. He died in 1832; he did not live to see his reasoning corrected.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

Carnot’s result: \(\eta = 1 - T_C/T_H\) for a reversible engine. Carnot did not have absolute temperature; he used the temperature of the steam point, etc., but the functional form was correct. His reasoning is independent of the caloric model’s physical truth — it is a logical argument about reversibility and perpetual motion.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern efficiency formula treats heat as energy transfer and temperature as absolute. Carnot’s argument is about the logical impossibility of perpetual motion and the reversibility condition. The modern form obscures the fact that his result is a theorem about ideal machines, not a law about energy.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Dismissing Carnot because he used caloric; his logical deduction is independent of the underlying model of heat.
- Incorrectly replacing “caloric” with “entropy” — this is epistemically false. Caloric is conserved in his model; entropy is not. The correct modern analog is the quantity of heat/energy passing through the engine.
- Confusing his “fall of caloric” with temperature drop; the proportionality holds, but the concepts differ.
- Trying to find modern isothermal and adiabatic labels; Carnot’s description is phenomenological.
