# Protocol: Isaac Newton – *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687), Axioms

**Chapter:** 5 – Force and Motion—I  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Comfort with vectors and the modern statement of Newton’s three laws.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Cohen & Whitman translation (1999) – clear modern English with extensive notes. Not free.
- **Free alternative:** Motte translation (1729) – archaic language, some passages ambiguous. [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_(1729))
- **Translation notes:** The Motte translation uses "quantity of motion" for momentum and "impressed force" for impulse. Use the Cohen & Whitman edition for critical passages if possible.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Universal gravitational constant G | Newton expresses everything as proportion, never as an equation with a constant |
| Vector notation | “Quantity of motion” (momentum) and force are directed magnitudes, manipulated geometrically, not algebraically |
| Field concept | Force is action-at-a-distance or contact; there is no mediating field |
| Inertial frame as a defined concept | Law I assumes absolute space and time; the modern inertial-frame definition is implicit, not explicit |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Write down the modern three laws from memory in vector form. Then set them aside — you will not find them verbatim in Newton.
- [ ] **Primary source:** Open Book I of the *Principia*. Read the Definitions first (mass, momentum, force, etc.). Notice Newton’s careful, non-mathematical language.
- [ ] **Regulae Philosophandi:** Read the Rules of Reasoning in Philosophy at the start of Book III if your edition includes them, or seek them in the preliminaries. These four rules are the epistemological foundation of Newton’s method.
- [ ] **Axioms:** Read Law I, Law II, Law III and each of the corollaries that follow.
- [ ] **Close reading:** For Law II, pay attention to the phrase *“mutationem motus proportionalem esse vi motrici impressae”* — change of motion is proportional to the impressed motive force. Modern \(F = ma\) is not directly stated; it is implied in the limit of equal time intervals for constant mass.
- [ ] **Rewrite:** After reading, rewrite each law in modern vector form and verify your translation against the original Latin (if possible). Note what is added and what is lost.
- [ ] **Corollary I:** Work through Corollary I (the parallelogram of forces) using vector addition. Recognize that Newton proves this geometrically.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Identify what Newton is arguing against — Cartesian vortex theory and the impetus tradition.
- [ ] **Reception task:** The *Principia* was immediately recognized as a monumental work, but its mathematical difficulty limited its audience. Continental mathematicians (Leibniz, Bernoulli) reformulated it in calculus terms over the following decades.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

Newton’s Second Law: \(\Delta(mv) \propto F\Delta t\). For constant mass, it reduces to \(F = ma\). The Third Law is strictly about action and reaction in contact/collision contexts; Newton’s own examples are collisions and pressure.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern vector equation treats force as a field quantity that can be summed algebraically at a point. Newton’s “impressed force” is an impulse — a discrete event, not a continuous field. The modern form obscures the event-like, transactional nature of force in Newton’s mechanics.

## 5. Secondary Sources

- Richard Westfall, *Never at Rest* – biography placing the work in context.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Treating “quantity of motion” (momentum) as a scalar; Newton treats it as a directed magnitude in the Corollaries.
- Assuming Law III implies forces always occur in equal pairs for field interactions without contact — Newton’s own examples are collisions and pressure.
- Getting lost in the geometric arguments; focus on the logical structure, not the Euclidean style.
- Skipping the Definitions and *Regulae*; they are not optional preamble — they are the epistemological framework.

## 8. Epistemic Safety Checklist (Post-Reading)

- [ ] I can state Newton’s laws in his own vocabulary (“quantity of motion,” “impressed force,” etc.).
- [ ] I can identify at least one tool Newton lacked that I used to understand his result.
- [ ] I can describe how the *Principia* was received — its immediate recognition but limited mathematical audience.
- [ ] I can name what the modern vector formalism obscures about Newton’s argument.
- [ ] I have not used the phrase “basically the same as” or “just a primitive version of” in my notes.
