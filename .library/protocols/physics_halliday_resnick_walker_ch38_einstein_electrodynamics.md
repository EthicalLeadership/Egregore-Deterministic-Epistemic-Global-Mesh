# Protocol: Albert Einstein – *On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies* (1905)

**Chapter:** 38 – Relativity  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Galilean relativity, Maxwell’s equations (conceptual), Lorentz transformation.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Perrett and Jeffery translation, from *The Principle of Relativity* (1923).
- **Free access:** [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Electrodynamics_of_Moving_Bodies)
- **Translation notes:** The translation is clear and modern; Einstein’s original is in German.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Spacetime interval formalism | Einstein derives the Lorentz transformation from postulates, not from metric invariance |
| Four-vectors | Velocity, momentum, and force are not unified into a four-dimensional framework |
| Minkowski geometry | The geometric interpretation of relativity (1908) is not present; Einstein uses algebra |
| Concept of proper time as invariant | His \(\tau\) is the time coordinate in the moving frame (what we now call \(t'\)), not the invariant proper time |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Recall the two postulates of special relativity. Write down the Lorentz transformation equations from memory.
- [ ] **Primary source:** Read Part I: Kinematics. Start with the definition of simultaneity — Einstein’s operational method using light signals. This is the conceptual core.
- [ ] **Derive:** Work through his derivation of the Lorentz transformation (Section 3). Follow the algebra step by step.
- [ ] **Consequences:** Note how he derives time dilation and length contraction from the transformation.
- [ ] **Critical note:** Einstein uses \(\tau\) for the time coordinate in the moving reference frame (\(t'\)), not the invariant proper time. Proper time is the spacetime interval \(ds/c\), a concept that emerges later.
- [ ] **Electrodynamics:** Read Part II: Electrodynamics only if you’re comfortable with Maxwell’s equations; otherwise, skip for now, but appreciate that the transformation of the fields is what motivated the paper.
- [ ] **Compare:** After reading, compare Einstein’s derivation to the modern textbook treatment (usually based on the invariance of the spacetime interval).
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Identify what Einstein is arguing against — the Lorentz-Poincaré interpretation of the Lorentz transformation as a mathematical convenience without physical meaning.
- [ ] **Reception task:** The paper was not immediately celebrated; it was one of several papers in 1905. Recognition grew gradually, especially after Minkowski’s geometric reformulation (1908) and experimental confirmations.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

The Lorentz transformation: \(x' = \gamma(x - vt)\), \(t' = \gamma(t - vx/c^2)\). Einstein showed they followed from the constancy of the speed of light and the relativity principle.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern derivation from the invariance of the spacetime interval treats the Lorentz transformation as a rotation in Minkowski space. Einstein’s derivation is operational and algebraic — built from light-signal definitions of simultaneity. The modern form obscures the epistemological priority of measurement in his reasoning.

## 5. Secondary Sources

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Getting lost in the notation: Einstein uses \(\tau\) for moving-frame time, \(V\) for relative speed. Do not confuse \(\tau\) with proper time.
- Assuming the paper is hard to read because of the subject; the kinematics part is actually quite accessible with high school algebra.
- Ignoring the operational definitions — they are crucial to the logical structure.
- Treating the modern spacetime-interval derivation as “the” derivation; Einstein’s operational derivation is logically independent and historically prior.

## 7. Follow-up / Cross-references

- Einstein’s 1916 general relativity paper.

- [ ] I can name what the modern spacetime-interval derivation obscures about Einstein’s operational reasoning.
