# Protocol: Ernest Rutherford – *The Scattering of α and β Particles by Matter and the Structure of the Atom* (1911)

**Chapter:** 44 – Nuclear Physics  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Conservation of energy and angular momentum, Coulomb force, basic scattering concepts.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** *Philosophical Magazine* (1911). Original in English.
- **Free access:** [Internet Archive (Philosophical Magazine)](https://archive.org/details/philosophicalma00goog)
- **Translation notes:** No translation needed.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Quantum mechanics | The scattering is purely classical; the same formula emerges in quantum mechanics, but Rutherford did not know why |
| Nuclear structure beyond point charge | He treats the nucleus as a massive, positively charged point; protons and neutrons are unknown |
| Explanation for atomic stability | He explicitly acknowledges the “death spiral” problem: classical electrons would radiate and crash |
| Strong/weak nuclear forces | The nucleus is held together by Coulomb repulsion alone in his model; he does not explain nuclear cohesion |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Recall the Geiger–Marsden experiment: alpha particles fired at thin gold foil; a few bounced back. Sketch the setup.
- [ ] **Primary source:** Read the paper, focusing on the derivation of the scattering formula.
- [ ] **Classical derivation:** Rutherford uses classical mechanics: the alpha particle follows a hyperbolic trajectory under a \(1/r^2\) repulsive force. Work through the geometry and the derivation of the scattering angle in terms of impact parameter.
- [ ] **Famous formula:** Note how he arrives at the famous formula: \(N(\theta) \propto 1/\sin^4(\theta/2)\).
- [ ] **Conclusion:** Understand his conclusion: the large deflections require a massive, positively charged nucleus concentrated in a tiny volume.
- [ ] **Death spiral:** Identify where Rutherford acknowledges the incompleteness of his model — the “death spiral” problem. He does not claim to have solved atomic structure; he claims to have established the nuclear scattering framework.
- [ ] **Solve:** After reading, solve a simple scattering problem using Rutherford’s formula to see how it compares with experimental data.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Identify what Rutherford is arguing against — the Thomson “plum pudding” model, where positive charge is diffuse and alpha particles should pass through with minimal deflection.
- [ ] **Reception task:** The nuclear model was accepted as a scattering framework, but the stability problem was acknowledged immediately. Bohr’s 1913 model was the first attempt to resolve it.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

Rutherford scattering cross-section:

\[
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \left( \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{4E} \right)^2 \frac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)}
\]

This formula remains valid in quantum mechanics (coincidentally).

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern formula treats the nucleus as a quantum-mechanical object and the scattering as a wave process. Rutherford’s derivation is purely classical Coulomb scattering. The modern form obscures the fact that Rutherford’s model was explicitly incomplete — he knew it could not explain stability, and he presented it as a scattering law, not a complete atomic theory.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- The derivation uses classical mechanics; later quantum mechanics yields the same result, but Rutherford’s original is purely classical.
- Not appreciating that the nuclear model creates the “death spiral” problem — electrons would radiate and crash into the nucleus — which Bohr later resolved. This incompleteness is not a failure; it is a productive boundary condition.
- Treating the nucleus as a point; actually it has a finite size, but that wasn’t known yet.
- Assuming Rutherford claimed to have solved atomic structure; he claimed to have established the nuclear scattering law.
