# Protocol: Thomas Young – *On the Theory of Light and Colours* (1802) & *Experiments and Calculations Relative to Physical Optics* (1803)

**Chapter:** 35 – Interference  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Wave interference, double-slit experiment, coherent sources.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Young’s 1802 Bakerian lecture and 1803 paper, both in English.
- **Free access:** [Internet Archive (Philosophical Transactions)](https://archive.org/details/philosophicaltra00roya)
- **Translation notes:** No translation needed, but the vocabulary is early 19th-century English.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Transverse wave theory | Young initially assumed longitudinal waves (like sound); polarization remained unexplained until Fresnel |
| Wavelength measurement precision | His estimates were qualitative or semi-quantitative |
| Corpuscular theory refutation | He argued by analogy and experiment, not by falsification in the modern sense |
| Concept of coherence as a formal condition | He recognized the need for similar sources but did not formalize coherence |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Sketch the double-slit setup and predict the fringe pattern. Write the condition for constructive interference.
- [ ] **1802 lecture:** Read the 1802 lecture first. Pay attention to where Young describes the superposition of waves and introduces the principle of interference.
- [ ] **Note:** In 1802, Young uses two closely spaced pinholes/apertures and water-wave analogies. He does not yet describe the precise double-slit experiment with measured fringes.
- [ ] **1803 paper:** Now read the 1803 paper, *Experiments and Calculations Relative to Physical Optics*. This is where the iconic double-slit experiment with precise fringe measurements appears.
- [ ] **Wave analogy:** Young makes an analogy to water waves and sound; note how he uses that analogy to argue that light is a wave.
- [ ] **Derive:** Derive the condition for constructive interference (\(d \sin\theta = m\lambda\)) from his description, even though he didn’t use that formula.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Identify what Young is arguing against — Newton’s corpuscular theory, which was the dominant optical theory in Britain.
- [ ] **Reception task:** Young’s wave theory was met with hostility in Britain (Newton’s authority was strong) and greater acceptance in France (Fresnel, Arago). It took two decades for wave optics to prevail.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

Young’s experiment is the first demonstration of wave interference of light, leading to the wave theory’s triumph. Modern version: \(\Delta x = \lambda L/d\).

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern formula treats light as an electromagnetic wave with a precisely measurable wavelength. Young’s argument is by analogy to water waves and sound, not by electromagnetic theory. The modern form obscures the analogical, phenomenological basis of his reasoning.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Attributing the double-slit experiment to the 1802 paper; the precise version is 1803.
- Expecting a detailed quantitative description; Young gave a qualitative argument with some quantitative estimates.
- Overlooking that Young initially assumed longitudinal waves (like sound), which couldn’t explain polarization; later Fresnel added transverse waves.

## 7. Follow-up / Cross-references

- Fresnel’s memoir on diffraction (recommended protocol, when added).
- Maxwell’s electromagnetic theory where light is a transverse wave.

## 8. Epistemic Safety Checklist (Post-Reading)

- [ ] I can distinguish between Young’s 1802 (pinholes, analogy) and 1803 (double-slit, measurement) contributions.
- [ ] I can identify at least one tool Young lacked that I used to understand his result.
- [ ] I can describe the hostile reception in Britain and the gradual acceptance of wave optics.
- [ ] I can name what the modern electromagnetic wave theory obscures about Young’s analogical reasoning.
- [ ] I have not used the phrase “basically the same as” or “just a primitive version of” in my notes.
