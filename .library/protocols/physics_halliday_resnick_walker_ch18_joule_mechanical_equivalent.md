# Protocol: James Prescott Joule – *On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat* (1845)

**Chapter:** 18 – Temperature, Heat, and the First Law  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Understanding of work, heat, and the First Law as energy conservation including thermal forms.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Original paper in English; no translation needed.
- **Free access:** [Royal Society](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstl.1845.0016)
- **Translation notes:** Joule’s units (foot-pounds per British thermal unit) are archaic; focus on proportional reasoning rather than exact numerical conversion.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| First Law of Thermodynamics as a formal statement | Joule shows equivalence, not conservation as a general law |
| Concept of internal energy | He treats heat as a state function of a material, not as energy transfer into microscopic modes |
| Statistical mechanics | He cannot explain why work becomes heat; he only measures the ratio |
| General acceptance of energy conservation | His result was one of several (Mayer, Colding, Helmholtz); the community was divided |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Write a paragraph explaining caloric theory: heat as a weightless fluid. Then predict what kind of experiment could challenge it.
- [ ] **Primary source:** Read the paper, focusing on the paddle-wheel experiment section.
- [ ] **Diagram:** Diagram the apparatus: falling weight → paddles churning water → temperature rise measured by thermometer.
- [ ] **Calculations:** Track Joule’s calculations: he measured temperature increase, converted to heat using the specific heat of water, and compared to the work done (weight × distance fallen).
- [ ] **Convert:** Convert his result (about 4.18 J/cal in modern terms) from his units (foot-pounds per British thermal unit).
- [ ] **Repetition:** Note that Joule repeated the experiment with different materials and methods to rule out calibration errors.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Identify what Joule is arguing against — caloric theory and the separation of heat and mechanical work as distinct substances.
- [ ] **Reception task:** Joule’s work provided crucial evidence, but acceptance was gradual and contested. Caloric theory persisted into the 1850s. Full acceptance required Helmholtz (1847), Clausius (1850), and the British Association’s endorsement.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

Joule’s fundamental relation establishes the equivalency of mechanical work and heat. The modern form \(\Delta U = Q - W\) is not yet present in his paper; he establishes the numerical ratio, not the general law.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern First Law treats heat as energy transfer and internal energy as a state function. Joule treats heat as a measurable effect of mechanical work on a material. The modern form obscures the experimental, material-specific nature of his demonstration.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Getting bogged down by archaic units; focus on the proportional reasoning.
- Claiming Joule’s experiment “disproved” caloric theory single-handedly; it was one contribution to a gradual shift.
- Forgetting that Joule’s experiment does not by itself prove conservation of energy (that required Helmholtz and Clausius).
- Not appreciating the experimental precision required to measure tiny temperature rises with 19th-century thermometers.

## 8. Epistemic Safety Checklist (Post-Reading)

- [ ] I can state Joule’s result in his own terms: mechanical work produces a proportional quantity of heat.
- [ ] I can identify at least one tool Joule lacked that I used to understand his result.
- [ ] I can describe the gradual, contested reception of his work — not its immediate canonization.
- [ ] I can name what the modern First Law obscures about Joule’s material-specific experimental reasoning.
- [ ] I have not used the phrase “basically the same as” or “just a primitive version of” in my notes.
