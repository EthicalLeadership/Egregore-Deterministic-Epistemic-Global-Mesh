# Protocol: Clinton Davisson & Lester Germer – *Diffraction of Electrons by a Crystal of Nickel* (1927)

**Chapter:** 2 – Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation  
**Textbook:** Griffiths – *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics*  
**Tier:** Recommended  
**Prerequisites:** de Broglie matter-wave hypothesis, Bragg’s law for X-ray diffraction, kinetic energy of electrons in terms of momentum, basic crystal planes.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Davisson & Germer, “Diffraction of Electrons by a Crystal of Nickel,” *Physical Review* 30, 705–740 (1927).
- **Free access:** [APS (DOI:10.1103/PhysRev.30.705)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.30.705)
- **Local copy:** `.library/source_papers/davisson_germer_1927.pdf`
- **Translation notes:** The paper is in English. The “accidental” recrystallization of the nickel target after a vacuum break is described in the experimental section; it was essential to observing sharp diffraction peaks.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Schrödinger equation as standard tool | The paper is contemporary with wave mechanics but treats electrons as classical matter waves diffracted by a crystal. |
| Born approximation / quantum scattering theory | The analysis uses Bragg diffraction, not a quantum-mechanical scattering cross section. |
| Wavefunction concept | Electrons are described as waves with wavelength λ = h/p, not as solutions of a wave equation with boundary conditions. |
| Modern surface science | The crystal structure of the nickel surface is inferred from the diffraction pattern; modern LEED apparatus did not exist. |
| Quantum theory of solids | Band structure, reciprocal lattice, and Bloch waves are not part of the analysis. |
| Spin and relativistic effects | The electrons are non-relativistic and spin is ignored. |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Write de Broglie’s relation λ = h/p and Bragg’s law nλ = 2d sin θ. For an electron accelerated through voltage V, compute p and λ.
- [ ] **Primary source:** Read the introduction and experimental setup. Note that the authors were initially studying electron scattering, not testing wave mechanics.
- [ ] **Close reading:** Find the section describing the accidental recrystallization and the appearance of discrete diffraction maxima.
- [ ] **Data analysis:** Follow the authors’ use of Bragg’s law to extract a wavelength from the observed angles and compare it with de Broglie’s prediction.
- [ ] **Derive:** For nickel, the relevant crystal spacing is d ≈ 0.215 nm. Show that the predicted diffraction angle for 54 eV electrons is consistent with the observed maximum.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Explain why this experiment was decisive: it converted de Broglie’s hypothesis from speculation into an empirically confirmed fact.
- [ ] **Reception task:** Note that G. P. Thomson independently confirmed electron diffraction around the same time using thin metal foils; together the two experiments established the wave nature of electrons.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

The modern analog is low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) and, more abstractly, the quantum-mechanical description of a particle encountering a periodic potential. The Schrödinger equation predicts that an electron wave of momentum p will be Bragg-reflected by crystal planes.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern treatment uses the Schrödinger equation, reciprocal lattice vectors, and scattering theory. Davisson and Germer used classical wave diffraction and an empirical crystal model. The step from “electrons have a wavelength” to “the electron is described by a wavefunction satisfying a wave equation” is not made in the paper.

## 5. Secondary Sources

- R. K. Gehrenbeck, “Electron diffraction: fifty years ago” (*Physics Today*, 1978) – historical overview of Davisson–Germer and Thomson.
- A. Tonomura, *The Quantum World Unveiled by Electron Waves* – modern electron-diffraction experiments and their interpretation.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Saying Davisson and Germer set out to test de Broglie’s hypothesis; they discovered the diffraction pattern while studying electron scattering.
- Treating the experiment as a direct test of the Schrödinger equation; it tests the wave nature of matter, not the specific form of the equation.
- Ignoring the role of the accidental crystal growth; the original polycrystalline target did not show sharp peaks.
- Confusing this with the double-slit experiment for electrons; Davisson–Germer uses crystal Bragg diffraction.

## 7. Follow-up / Cross-references

- de Broglie 1924 protocol (the matter-wave hypothesis that this experiment confirmed).
- Born 1926 scattering protocol (the quantum scattering theory that later explained such experiments from first principles).
- Schrödinger 1926 protocol (the wave equation whose solutions describe electrons in a crystal potential).
