# Protocol: Michael Faraday – *Experimental Researches in Electricity*, Series XXVIII–XXIX (1852)

**Chapter:** 21 – Electric Charge; 22 – Electric Fields  
**Textbook:** Halliday, Resnick, Walker – *Fundamentals of Physics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Vector field concepts, electric field lines, Gauss’s law.

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** *Experimental Researches in Electricity*, Vol. 1 and 3, public domain. Original in English.
- **Free access:** [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/experimentalres00faragoog)
- **Translation notes:** Faraday uses “specific inductive capacity,” not “dielectric constant” or “electric displacement.” Do not retroject Maxwell’s terminology.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Vector calculus | Faraday uses words, iron filings, and physical demonstration; there are no equations |
| Maxwell’s equations | The field concept is not yet mathematical; it is phenomenological |
| Dielectric constant / electric displacement | These are Maxwell’s concepts (1860s). Faraday uses “specific inductive capacity.” |
| Atomic theory of charge | He does not know about electrons; charge is a property of materials and their boundaries |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Draw the electric field lines for a point charge and a dipole from your textbook. Then set the textbook aside.
- [ ] **Primary source:** Read Series XXVIII and XXIX (1852). Focus on sections where Faraday describes induction in dielectrics and his “lines of inductive force.”
- [ ] **Physical demonstration:** Notice how Faraday physically demonstrates the existence of these lines using iron filings and charged conductors.
- [ ] **Reality of lines:** Pay attention to his argument that the lines of force are real physical entities, not mere mathematical conveniences — he insists the medium is strained.
- [ ] **Sketch:** Sketch his “lines of force” diagrams alongside your vector field plots; see the correspondence, but note the differences.
- [ ] **Critical note:** Faraday uses “specific inductive capacity” — do not retroject Maxwell’s “dielectric constant” or “electric displacement.”
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Identify what Faraday is arguing against — action-at-a-distance theories (Coulomb, Ampère) that treated force as direct interaction without medium.
- [ ] **Reception task:** Faraday’s visual, non-mathematical approach was respected but not fully mathematized until Maxwell (1861–1865). Continental physicists were skeptical of his “physical” lines.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

Faraday’s lines of force map directly to field lines; the density of lines is proportional to field strength. His ideas are the precursor to the field concept.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern field concept is a mathematical object defined by partial differential equations. Faraday’s lines are experimental phenomena demonstrated with iron filings and charged bodies. The modern form obscures the physical, medium-based ontology that motivated his entire research program.

## 5. Secondary Sources

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Expecting equations; Faraday uses words and pictures only.
- Retrojecting Maxwell’s “dielectric constant” or “electric displacement” onto Faraday; he used “specific inductive capacity.”
- Missing the crucial shift from action-at-a-distance to local action through the medium.
- Getting lost in experimental details; skim the less relevant preparations and focus on the conceptual conclusions.

## 8. Epistemic Safety Checklist (Post-Reading)

- [ ] I can state Faraday’s argument in his own terms: lines of force, specific inductive capacity, strained medium.
- [ ] I can identify at least one tool Faraday lacked that I used to understand his result.
- [ ] I can describe the skepticism toward his physical lines and their later mathematization by Maxwell.
- [ ] I can name what the modern field equations obscure about Faraday’s experimental, medium-based reasoning.
- [ ] I have not used the phrase “basically the same as” or “just a primitive version of” in my notes.
