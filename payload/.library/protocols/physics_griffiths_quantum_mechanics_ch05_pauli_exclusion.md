# Protocol: Wolfgang Pauli – *Über den Zusammenhang des Abschlusses der Elektronengruppen im Atom mit der Komplexstruktur der Spektren* (1925)

**Chapter:** 5 – Identical Particles  
**Textbook:** Griffiths – *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics*  
**Tier:** Essential  
**Prerequisites:** Atomic structure, electron configuration, the periodic table, angular momentum, basic quantum numbers, the helium atom (helpful).

## 1. Edition & Translation

- **Recommended:** Pauli’s 1925 paper, *Zeitschrift für Physik* 31, 765–783. English translation in B. L. van der Waerden (ed.), *Sources of Quantum Mechanics* (Dover, 1968).
- **Free access:** [Springer (DOI:10.1007/BF02980631)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02980631)
- **Translation notes:** Pauli introduces a “new quantum-theoretical property of the electron” later identified as spin. The exclusion principle is stated as a rule for electron groups, not yet as antisymmetry of the many-electron wavefunction.

## 2. What the Author Did NOT Have

| Author Did NOT Have | Consequence for Reading |
|---|---|
| Electron spin | Pauli introduces a fourth quantum number but does not identify it as intrinsic angular momentum; spin is proposed by Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck later in 1925. |
| Quantum mechanics / wavefunctions | The exclusion rule is phenomenological; there is no Schrödinger equation or many-electron wavefunction. |
| Antisymmetry principle | The statement “no two electrons can have the same set of quantum numbers” is not derived from ψ_A(x_1,…,x_N) changing sign under exchange. |
| Spin-statistics theorem | The connection between spin-½ and Fermi–Dirac statistics is not known; Fermi and Dirac’s statistical distributions come in 1926. |
| Magnetic spin quantum number m_s | Pauli’s fourth quantum number is not yet given the values ±½ in the modern sense. |

## 3. Reading Steps

- [ ] **Pre-reading:** Write the electron configurations for the first 18 elements (H to Ar) and note where the shell structure creates the periodic-table blocks (s, p, d).
- [ ] **Primary source:** Read Pauli’s summary of the anomalous Zeeman effect and complex spectra, which forced him to introduce a fourth quantum number.
- [ ] **Close reading:** Locate the statement of the exclusion principle: in an atom, no two electrons can share the same values of all four quantum numbers.
- [ ] **Shell structure:** Follow Pauli’s explanation of how the exclusion principle determines the closing of electron shells (2, 8, 18, …) and thus the structure of the periodic table.
- [ ] **Derive:** Show that the maximum number of electrons in a subshell with orbital quantum number l is 2(2l+1), and explain how Pauli’s rule predicts this before spin was understood.
- [ ] **Historical-context task:** Understand that Pauli was trying to solve the puzzles of the anomalous Zeeman effect and the building-up principle (Aufbau) of the periodic table without knowing about electron spin.
- [ ] **Reception task:** Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck identified the fourth quantum number as spin in late 1925; Dirac later derived the spin-statistics connection from relativistic quantum mechanics.

## 4. Modern Analog / Retrojection

The modern result is the Pauli exclusion principle for identical fermions: the total wavefunction must be antisymmetric under exchange of any two identical fermions.

**What is lost in this retrojection?** The modern principle is a consequence of spin-statistics and relativistic quantum field theory. Pauli’s original formulation is a phenomenological rule for atomic electrons; the deep reason why it holds (and why it applies to all half-integer-spin particles) was not available to him.

## 5. Secondary Sources

- C. P. Enz, *No Time to Be Brief: A Scientific Biography of Wolfgang Pauli* – detailed context.
- S. Tomonaga, *The Story of Spin* – accessible account of how spin and exclusion emerged.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- Saying Pauli discovered electron spin; he proposed a fourth quantum number, but spin as a mechanical property came from Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck.
- Treating the exclusion principle as an axiom of non-relativistic quantum mechanics; its full justification requires the spin-statistics theorem.
- Confusing the original shell-closing argument with the modern antisymmetry requirement.
